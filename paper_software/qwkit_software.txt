qwkit: an open, tested Python toolkit for quantum-well intersubband
optics, from self-consistent states to saturated waveguide conversion
                                                 Gracen Ownby

                           University of Oklahoma, Norman, Oklahoma, USA


                                                  14 October 2026



                                                      Abstract

          qwkit is a small, open-source Python package (MIT license) that computes the optical response of
      doped semiconductor quantum wells to infrared light, from the electron states of a layered structure to
      the harmonic power generated in a waveguide. Its core chains six calculations that are usually done
      with separate in-house codes: self-consistent Schrödinger–Poisson states with band nonparabolicity
      and depolarization; the resonant second-order susceptibility χ (2) for arbitrary subband populations; LO-
      phonon intersubband scattering rates; multilevel saturation; planar waveguide modes and electrode fields;
      and coupled-mode propagation with pump depletion and quasi-phase matching. Five further modules
      add exact parameter sensitivities of the self-consistent model by implicit differentiation; the collective
      (time-dependent Hartree, random-phase) linear and second-order response, with and without in-plane
      dispersion; the two-dimensional electron gas at a single polar heterointerface; and intersubband thermal
      emitters at elevated temperature. Each module is checked by automated tests against analytic limits, exact
      symmetries, conservation laws or a published value (58 tests). The package has been used for a complete
      design study of electrically reversible quasi-phase matching in symmetric triple quantum wells, whose
      every number and figure it regenerates, and for six companion studies. The whole chain, from layer list to
      conversion efficiency, runs in under ten seconds on one processor core, most of it in the waveguide mode
      solver.


1    Summary
Electrons confined in quantum wells (QWs) a few nanometres thick occupy discrete subbands, and transitions
between them, intersubband (ISB) transitions, can be placed anywhere in the mid- and far-infrared by choosing
layer thicknesses. Coupling two or three such transitions resonantly with a pump and its harmonic produces
second-order nonlinearities χ (2) that exceed those of bulk crystals by orders of magnitude [1–3]. These
nonlinearities drive difference-frequency terahertz sources in quantum cascade lasers [4], giant and electrically
tunable nonlinear metasurfaces [5, 6], and waveguide frequency converters [7]. Predicting what a given
structure will do requires a chain of calculations: subband states in the structure’s own electrostatic potential,
collective (depolarization) shifts of the transitions, the susceptibility for the actual subband populations, how
fast excited electrons relax, how the populations saturate under strong pumping, how the light is confined in a
waveguide, and how the harmonic builds up along it.

    qwkit implements this chain, and the extensions described below, in about 2500 lines of documented
Python that depend only on NumPy and SciPy. A user describes a structure as a list of layers, applies a
field and a doping level, and obtains energies, dipoles, χ (2) , absorption, scattering rates, saturation curves,
waveguide modes and conversion efficiencies, and, with the extension modules, parameter sensitivities,
collective spectra and thermal emission spectra, all through plain functions and dictionaries that can be
inspected at every stage.


2    Statement of need
Design studies of ISB nonlinear devices are often carried out with in-house codes, and the steps of the chain
are frequently reported separately: a band-structure calculation in one paper, a susceptibility estimate in
another, a propagation model with a single assumed relaxation time in a third. This makes results hard
to reproduce and hides model choices that change conclusions. Three examples arose while developing
the package. A susceptibility formula written with diagonal dipole elements measured from an arbitrary
origin produces a spurious χ (2) in a perfectly symmetric structure. Replacing a common 1 ps relaxation
time with level-resolved LO-phonon rates lowers absolute conversion efficiencies at watt-level pumping
by factors of 1.3–1.8 at 1.26 W and 1.2–4.2 at 10 W, depending on the phase-matching scheme. And an
“idealized” benchmark that keeps the on-state absorption in switched-off segments understates a competing,
Stark-modulated quasi-phase-matching (QPM) scheme by 1.6–25 times at 1.26–20 W [8]. An open, tested
implementation of the whole chain lets such choices be stated, varied and checked.
    The intended users are researchers and students who design or interpret ISB absorbers, emitters, mod-
ulators and nonlinear converters; those who want a transparent reference implementation of standard ISB
formulas [3]; and those who need a fast, scriptable forward model, with exact derivatives, for optimization or
uncertainty studies.


3    State of the field
Table 1 compares qwkit with widely used open and commercial codes for semiconductor heterostructures,
as their authors describe them. General-purpose packages solve the Schrödinger–Poisson problem in one
to three dimensions with multiband k · p models [9–11]; quantum-cascade-laser tools design laser active
regions [12]; and solar-cell libraries couple QW absorption to device transport [13, 14]. As far as their
publications and documentation describe, none of the open codes combines depolarization-shifted transitions,
a population-resolved resonant χ (2) , LO-phonon relaxation, multilevel saturation, waveguide modes and
saturable coupled-mode propagation in one tested chain. qwkit is deliberately narrower than the general-
purpose codes: it is a one-dimensional, single-conduction-band code for the ISB optics of electron-doped
structures (with a two-band Kane cross-check of its dipoles), and it does not replace multiband k · p or
transport solvers.

4      Software design
4.1    Architecture
The package has eleven modules (Fig. 1): six that form the chain from layer list to conversion efficiency, and
five extensions that use the same states and conventions. Each is a set of plain functions operating on NumPy
arrays and dictionaries, so any intermediate quantity can be inspected, modified or replaced. The few classes
hold precomputed data: propagation.Medium holds a saturable medium built from a “state set” dictio-
nary (energies, dipoles, shifted transition energies, populations, density), which sp.state_set produces
from a self-consistent solution; tdh.TDH and krpa.KRPA hold the Hartree kernel and unperturbed density
matrix of a response calculation; and sens.Model holds the parameter vector of the differentiable model.

4.2    Physics and numerical choices
States. The Schrödinger equation with the BenDaniel–Duke operator [16] and Kane nonparabolicity
m∗ (z, E) = medge (z)[1 + (E − V (z))/Eg (z)] [17], with InGaAs/InAlAs band parameters from Ref. [18], is
discretized on a cell-centred grid and solved with a tridiagonal eigensolver. Two choices make symmetric
structures exactly symmetric numerically, which matters because a field-induced χ (2) is a small difference
of large terms. First, no grid node sits on an interface, so mirror-symmetric stacks give mirror-symmetric
matrices. Second, each nonparabolic state is found by index tracking: the k-th state satisfies E = the k-th
eigenvalue of H(E), which preserves node counting, whereas matching states by nearest energy can swap or
duplicate them. States from different energy-dependent Hamiltonians are not exactly orthogonal; they are
Löwdin-orthonormalized [19], which preserves parity and changes them minimally, together with at least
ten box states above the highest localized one (solved at the converged potential if needed), and only then
truncated to the states a calculation keeps (core.lowdin_localized, used by sp.prepare; sens
and thermal orthonormalize all their model states before selecting). The buffer matters when few states
are localized: the topmost localized state cannot be orthogonalized against states left out of the set. As an
exact alternative, core.kane_dipoles forms dipoles from the two-component states of the two-band

Waveguide, electrodes and propagation. TM modes of planar multilayer guides with complex permit-
tivities (InP dispersion [23], Drude free carriers, gold [24]) are found by finite differences for Hy . Fields
under alternating-polarity electrodes come from a Fourier-series solution of Laplace’s equation for a uniform
permittivity, and the QPM coefficient is the first Fourier component of χ (2) (F(x)) [25]. Mode amplitudes
are propagated with fourth-order Runge–Kutta, including pump depletion, saturable losses and either a
periodic QPM pattern (propagate) or a sequence of different media, each one coherence length long
(propagate_segments), which describes Stark on/off QPM realistically.

Sensitivities. sens gives exact first-order derivatives of the one-period self-consistent model with respect
to layer thicknesses, field, doping, band offset, masses, nonparabolicity gaps and permittivities, by implicit
differentiation of the converged Hartree fixed point. Interfaces are smoothed by an error function one grid
cell wide, so every material profile is differentiable in the interface positions. State tangents follow from
first-order perturbation theory of the nonparabolic eigenproblems at fixed total density. They are computed
matrix-free: reduced resolvents are applied by shifted tridiagonal LU solves with projection and iterative
refinement, and the Hartree feedback (I − K) δVH = b is solved by GMRES (typically five iterations), so
neither a full eigendecomposition nor an explicit density-response matrix is formed (the dense route remains
as an option and agrees to 10−8 ). The closed-form post-processing (Löwdin step over all model states, about
ten more than are kept, then dipoles, depolarization shifts, χ (2) , absorption and a waveguide figure of merit)
is differentiated along each tangent by Richardson-extrapolated central differences, on the most localized
states rather than the lowest ones, so a delocalized box state is never selected.

Collective response. tdh solves the density-matrix equations of one period with the time-dependent
Hartree (random-phase) potential of the induced charge, the drive being referenced to the displacement field.
It returns χ (1) and χ (2) by frequency-domain perturbation theory and integrates the full nonlinear response in
real time (fourth-order Runge–Kutta). For two levels its kernel reproduces Ando’s depolarization shift, and
without the kernel the density-matrix χ (2) above is recovered. krpa generalizes it from subband populations
to Fermi occupations on in-plane wavevector shells with nonparabolic dispersions and a frequency-dependent
(polar-phonon) background permittivity, for linear and second-order response; only pairs with an occupation
difference carry first-order coherences, and an adiabatic local exchange–correlation kernel is optional. With a
single shell it reduces exactly to tdh.

Heterointerface and thermal emitters. interface computes the two-dimensional electron gas at a
single polar heterointerface self-consistently: Kane-nonparabolic subbands with an in-plane dispersion and
density of states consistent with the same Hamiltonian, Fermi-level pinning by compensating acceptors over a
depletion layer, a positive sheet at the interface and the cap surface or a fixed interface sheet (electric-double-
layer gating), an optional fixed ionized-donor density in the cap (for example oxygen vacancies created
by an electrolyte) with the remaining positive charge placed on the cap surface, at the interface or in the
donors, and optional Hedin–Lundqvist exchange–correlation, with Anderson mixing. thermal supplies
temperature-dependent material data (Varshni gaps, Γ–L separations, L-valley masses), intrinsic densities,
the self-consistent period at temperature with continuum states and an L-valley reservoir, ISB absorption
with thermal populations and k-resolved in-plane nonparabolicity, LO-phonon linewidths, an Urbach-tail
interband background, coupled-mode emissivity, generation leakage and two-surface thermophotovoltaic
(TPV) detailed balance.

4.3   Design trade-offs
The package favours transparency and verifiability over generality. A one-band model with Kane nonparabol-
icity describes conduction-band ISB transitions in narrow- and moderate-gap III–V wells well [17, 26] and
makes every matrix element inspectable; valence-subband and interband problems are out of scope until
a multiband module exists (thermal includes interband absorption only as a bulk Urbach tail). Plain
functions and dictionaries were chosen over an object hierarchy so that a study can stop after any step, swap
in a different model, and keep its inputs and outputs as JSON. Units are fixed per module (Å and eV in the
state, interface and response solvers, pm/V for their χ (2) ; SI for media in propagation, with waveguide
inputs in µm and cm−1 ; metres in electrodes) and are listed in the user guide. Steady-state rate equations
assume pulses much longer than the picosecond relaxation times, and the waveguide is planar; both are stated
limitations rather than hidden ones.


5     Validation
Each module is checked against independent references (Table 2); the 58 tests run in about 40 s on one
core. Fig. 2 shows four of them. Subband energies of a finite InGaAs/InAlAs well agree with the analytic

BenDaniel–Duke solution to 0.04 meV on the default 0.5 Å grid, with second-order convergence (Fig. 2a).
In a self-consistent mirror-symmetric triple well, |χ (2) (F) + χ (2) (−F)| stays below 4 × 10−11 |χ (2) | at every
field (Fig. 2b), more than four orders of magnitude below the test tolerance. With the parameters of a
published reproduction of Ferreira and Bastard’s calculation [15, 22] (m∗ = 0.07, conduction-band offset
0.2138 eV, εs = 12.5, ℏωLO = 36 meV, T = 0; that source does not state ε∞ , for which we take 10.89), the
LO-phonon emission time of a 10 nm GaAs well is 0.73 ps, against the published 0.83 ps (Fig. 2c). The 13%
difference is of the size produced by plausible choices of ε∞ (a value of 11.07 reproduces 0.83 ps), so this test
guards the magnitude of the rates rather than fixing their last digit. The coupled-mode solver reproduces the
phase-mismatched sinc2 solution to 10−6 and the (2/π)2 factor of sign-reversal QPM (Fig. 2d), and, with 256
steps per coherence length, conserves total power to 10−6 in a strongly depleted lossless case. The extension
modules are checked in the same way: the sensitivities against central finite differences, including cells where
the energy-dependent mass reaches its floor, and the matrix-free against the dense tangents; the collective
response against the single-particle χ (2) , Ando’s shift and its own real-time integration; krpa against tdh;
and the interface and thermal modules against closed-form densities, Gauss’s law, the Stefan–Boltzmann law
and detailed-balance limits.
    Corrections made after the first release change results slightly. The ends of the simulation box were
zero-flux (Neumann) boundaries, although documented as hard walls; they are now Dirichlet (ψ = 0 one
step beyond each end). Bound states of padded structures are unchanged (by less than 10−4 meV for 100 Å
pads), while continuum-like box states shift and spurious states localized at the box edges disappear. The
nonparabolic states are now Löwdin-orthonormalized together with at least ten box states above the localized
ones before the basis is truncated. Orthonormalizing an already truncated set gave its topmost states spurious
dipoles (up to 3% of the 1 → n oscillator strength in a capped basis of the heterointerface study), and so
did orthonormalizing the localized states alone when only a few are localized: for the 64/10/29 Å double
well in a 200 Å box, with three localized states, z13 came out 8.77 Å against 8.22 Å from the two-band
Kane states, and |χ (2) | about 8% too large. The two-band Kane states provide this independent check of
the Löwdin step: for six asymmetric double wells and the symmetric triple well at zero bias, the localized
two-component states overlap by less than 10−4 (against 0.04–0.07 for the raw one-band states); with the
buffered step the one-band dipoles agree with the Kane dipoles to within 0.7% and do not depend on the box
size, and computing the peak ground-state χ (2) of the double wells with the Kane dipoles changes it by −0.2
to −0.6%. The studies built on qwkit were re-run after these corrections.


6    Research use and performance
qwkit was built for, and used in, a complete design study of electrically reversible QPM in mirror-symmetric
InGaAs/InAlAs triple quantum wells [8]. The study’s scripts, distributed with the package, regenerate
every number and figure of that manuscript from the layer list: self-consistent model ladders and error
budgets, LO-phonon-resolved saturation, waveguide and electrode design, and propagation comparisons
against bipolar-biased, Stark-modulated and growth-reversed benchmarks. Fig. 3 shows three stages of
that workflow. The extension modules were developed for six companion studies whose scripts are also
distributed: saturation limits of resonant ISB second-harmonic generation [27], collective effects in the
resonant χ (2) (tdh) [28], temperature limits of bias-switchable ISB thermal emitters (thermal) [29], ISB
optics of the polar LaInO3 /BaSnO3 electron gas (interface, krpa) [30], electrolyte gating of the same
interface (interface) [31], and sensitivities, uncertainty quantification and growth-tolerant design (sens)

than through the displacement, which broke continuity of D at dielectric interfaces (changing |χ (2) | by at
most 0.1%); and a Runge–Kutta stage that sampled the next domain’s QPM sign at domain edges.
    On one core of a 2.8 GHz Xeon, a self-consistent solution with seven states on a 0.5 Å grid takes about
0.4 s, the LO-phonon rate matrix a few milliseconds, and a saturable, pump-depleted propagation over 500 µm
(about 700 steps) about 0.15 s. The TM mode of the waveguide, a dense eigenproblem on a 0.02 µm grid,
takes about 4 s per wavelength, and is needed once per waveguide design. The sensitivities of a self-consistent
solution with respect to all 12–14 parameters of a double or triple well cost about a fifth of its forward solve
(0.13–0.22 s). Parameter scans of thousands of designs are therefore practical on a workstation.

 7              Limitations and development plan
 qwkit treats conduction-band ISB physics in one dimension with a single band; the two-band Kane states
 serve only as a cross-check of the dipoles. Three of the extensions planned at the first release are now included:
 thermal subband populations and linewidths for high-temperature ISB emitters (thermal), exact derivatives
 for gradient-based design under growth tolerances (sens), and a collective (multisubband-plasmon) response
 beyond first-order depolarization (tdh, krpa). The next planned extension is an interband and carrier-escape
 module (hole subbands, interband absorption, tunnelling and thermionic escape) for QW solar cells and
 thermophotovoltaic absorbers. The saturable propagation still uses first-order depolarization, Lorentzian
 lines and LO-phonon relaxation only. New alloys can be registered with add_material; the padding
 material, the density-of-states mass, the depolarization permittivity and the LO-phonon parameters are
 function arguments, so other III–V systems need only parameter sets and validation; interface and krpa
 accept several polar-phonon modes and have been applied to a polar oxide, while nonpolar or strongly
 anisotropic materials need new scattering models.


 8              Availability
The version described here is release 0.6.1; its changes since 0.5.0 are listed in CHANGELOG.md. It requires
Python ≥3.10, NumPy and SciPy (Matplotlib for figures). It installs with pip install . and is released
under the MIT license. The source code, tests, documentation (a user guide with module reference and
worked examples), the scripts that regenerate this paper’s figures and the design studies are supplied with this
article; a public repository and an archived release with a persistent identifier will accompany publication.
Contributions and issue reports are welcome under the guidelines in CONTRIBUTING.md.
